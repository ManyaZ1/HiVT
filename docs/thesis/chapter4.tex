\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:method}

This chapter develops the methodology of this thesis. As
it was established, inspired by the compute asymmetry problem and the widespread
use of knowledge distillation in this domain, the thesis proposes a knowledge distillation
method for mixture-based trajectory predictors. The model used in this thesis
is HiVT: Hierarchical Vector Transformer for Multi-Agent Motion Prediction~\cite{hivt}, 
selected for its high performance and its ability to be trained
on a single GPU. In this chapter, the methodology is presented in detail
(Section~\ref{sec:method}): it firstly explores why a naive, index-aligned
distillation of HiVT's mixture output would fail, then derives a
permutation-invariant objective and its two variants, and finally describes the
implementation that makes the distillation signal reach the student reliably. The
experimental setup used to evaluate the method---dataset, metrics, and training
protocol---is deferred to Chapter~\ref{ch:impl}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{HiVT's Mixture Output and Training Loss}
\label{ssec:method_prelim}
As established in Section~\ref{ssec:sota_hivt}, for every agent, HiVT outputs 
a mixture of $K$=6 candidate trajectories {$\mu_k$} with per-step scales 
{$\scale_k$} and probability weights {$\pi_k$}. 
We can interpret this output as a predictive distribution over the future, where 
each trajectory is written as $\mathbf{y}\in\R^{2H}$, 
where $H$ is the number of prediction steps. The predicted distribution
is a Laplace mixture
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mixture}
  p(\mathbf{y}) \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\,
      \Lap\!\left(\mathbf{y}\mid \loc_k, \scale_k\right),
  \qquad \pi_k \ge 0,\ \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k = 1,
\end{equation}
where mode $k$ has location $\loc_k\in\R^{2H}$ (the trajectory itself), per-step
scale $\scale_k\in\R^{2H}_{>0}$ (its predicted uncertainty), and mixing
coefficient $\pi_k$. The components are the factorised, per-step Laplace densities
of Section~\ref{ssec:bg_mdn}, with $\Lap(y\mid\mu,b)=\tfrac{1}{2b}\exp(-|y-\mu|/b)$.

HiVT is trained with a regression term and a classification term. The regression
term is the Laplace negative log-likelihood (Section~\ref{ssec:bg_mle}) of the
ground-truth trajectory $\mathbf{y}^\star$ under the \emph{single best-matching}
mode. This defines a \emph{winner-takes-all} loss that, on each example, selects the mode
$k^\star$ closest to the ground truth and updates only its location and scale:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:reg}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}
    = -\frac{1}{H}\sum_{t=1}^{H}
       \log \Lap\!\left(\mathbf{y}^\star_t \mid \loc_{k^\star,t},\scale_{k^\star,t}\right),
  \qquad
  k^\star = \arg\min_k \sum_{t=1}^{H} \lVert \mu_{k,t} - y_t^\star \rVert_2
\end{equation}
A classification term then trains the mixing coefficients $\{\pi_k\}$ toward a
\emph{soft} target rather than a hard label on the winner: each mode is assigned a
target probability proportional to $\exp(-\bar{d}_k)$, where $\bar{d}_k$ is mode
$k$'s mean per-step displacement from the ground truth, and the predicted weights
are matched to this (stop-gradient) distribution by a cross-entropy loss.
Geometrically closer modes thus receive proportionally higher probability, with
$k^\star$ the most favoured. The per-step NLL expands to
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:laplace_nll}
  -\log\Lap(y\mid\mu,b) = \log(2b) + \frac{|y-\mu|}{b},
\end{equation}
a form we return to in Section~\ref{ssec:method_calib}.

\subsection{The Mode-Permutation Problem}
\label{ssec:method_perm}
The winner-takes-all loss in~\eqref{eq:reg} supervises only one mode per example,
chosen by proximity to the ground truth. It therefore never constrains \emph{which
slot} a given behaviour occupies: across the dataset, mode index $3$ of one trained
model might capture ``turn left'' while index $3$ of another captures ``go
straight.'' The loss can be considered \emph{symmetric under relabelling} of the
$K$ modes and  permuting the mode indices would leave it unchanged. Because of 
this symmetry, nothing during training pins a behaviour to a particular slot: 
the slot it occupies is fixed only by random initialisation and optimisation noise.  
Therefore, two independently trained HiVTs have no mechanism to agree on a slot ordering.
We verify this directly for the teacher HiVT-128 -- student HiVT-32 pair used in this work below (Fig.~\ref{fig:perm}).


The non determinism of the slots is fatal for any distillation term that pairs student 
and teacher modes naively \emph{by index}. 
Such a term would, for most scenes, compare a student mode against
an unrelated teacher mode---``turn left'' supervised toward ``go straight''---and
inject structured noise that the model cannot resolve. Empirically, the optimal
matching between a teacher's and a student's modes is far from the identity: the
permutation that best aligns them (computed per scene by linear assignment) almost
never maps slot $k$ to slot $k$, as the assignment matrix in
Figure~\ref{fig:perm} shows. Any correct distillation objective must therefore be
\emph{invariant to the ordering of both models' modes}.

One might hope to sidestep the slot non-determinism by replacing index pairing with
a \emph{similarity} pairing---matching each teacher mode to its closest student mode
(e.g.\ by linear assignment) and supervising the matched pairs. This is a strict
improvement over index pairing, but it carries its own costs: the hard assignment is
non-differentiable and can flip discontinuously during training, and a one-to-one 
matching is only defined when the two models carry the 
\emph{same} number of modes. When $K_T \neq K_S$, a matching that strictly compares
slots necessarily
leaves modes unmatched or double-counted, so the teacher's mode count is silently
forced onto the student. A set-to-mixture cross-entropy avoids this entirely: it is
defined for any $K_S$ and $K_T$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:perm}), letting student and
teacher differ freely in mode count.

\subsection{The Design Space of Distillation Objectives}
\label{ssec:method_design}
The permutation argument of Section~\ref{ssec:method_perm} is a \emph{constraint},
not yet a method: it rules out any objective that pairs modes by index, but several
families of permutation-respecting objectives remain. Before deriving the one this
thesis adopts, we lay out the design space and say why each alternative is rejected,
so that the proposed loss is understood as a deliberate choice among known options
rather than the only conceivable one. Four families are summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:design}; we take them in turn.

\paragraph{(i) Index-aligned soft-label KD.} The textbook approach---transfer the
teacher's output by matching it to the student's element-wise, as in
temperature-softmax distillation~\cite{hinton}---pairs student mode
$k$ with teacher mode $k$ and regresses one onto the other. This is the
implicit-identity matching that Section~\ref{ssec:method_perm} refutes: because the
two models number their modes in unrelated orders (Figure~\ref{fig:perm}), it
compares ``turn left'' against ``go straight'' on most scenes and injects
structured noise. It is the natural baseline and the one the whole method is
designed to avoid.

\paragraph{(ii) Assignment-based matching (Hungarian / optimal transport).}
Instead of trusting the index order, one can \emph{compute} a meaningful
correspondence: for each scene, find the pairing of teacher modes to student modes
that minimises a matching cost (typically the distance between their predicted
locations), and then supervise only the matched pairs. This is a linear-assignment
(Hungarian) or optimal-transport problem, and it is exactly how set-prediction
models such as DETR align their predictions to the ground truth~\cite{carion2020detr}.
It is the natural and rigorous fix to the index-pairing bug: by matching like with
like, it stops comparing ``turn left'' against ``go straight'', and the result is
\emph{permutation invariant}---relabelling the teacher's modes does not change the
loss. For these reasons it is a genuine, strict improvement over family~(i).

This method was not adopted in the end, because the hard assignment carries costs that
the density objective of families~(iii) and~(iv) avoids.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{It is expensive.} Solving the assignment is an $O(K^3)$ operation
    \emph{per scene}, repeated every training step.
  \item \textbf{It makes the training signal discontinuous.} The matching is an
    $\argmin$---a hard, discrete choice of \emph{which} pairing wins. This in itself
    is not fatal: like DETR~\cite{carion2020detr}, one simply treats the chosen
    pairing as fixed and backpropagates through the matched pairs, which gives a
    perfectly usable gradient. The real cost is that this choice is a step function
    of the predictions. Because the assignment is winner-take-all, a tiny weight
    update can make a \emph{different} pairing cheapest, and the whole correspondence
    then snaps to a new configuration in a single step. A teacher mode that sits
    almost equidistant between two student modes may be matched to one on this step
    and to the other on the next, so the gradient the optimiser computes is suddenly
    for a different objective than the one it was descending---the loss can even rise
    across a step that should have improved it. The surface the gradient lives on is
    thus locally smooth but stitched from pieces that swap discontinuously: jagged,
    and a source of training noise. A density loss has no hard decision to flip, so
    the supervision glides smoothly as the modes move.
\end{itemize}
A third limitation is decisive on its own: a hard one-to-one matching pairs each
teacher mode with exactly one student mode, so it is only well defined when the two
models carry the same number of modes ($K_T=K_S$). In a case where the counts would
differ, for example a six-mode teacher distilling into a four-mode student,
some modes would be left
unmatched or reused, and the teacher's mode count is silently forced onto the student. 
The density objective sidesteps every one of these costs (Section~\ref{ssec:method_pi}).
The optimal assignment is computed only as a \emph{diagnostic}
to visualise the permutation gap (Figure~\ref{fig:perm}), never as the training
signal.

\paragraph{(iii)--(iv) Density matching, and the direction of divergence.} The
alternative this thesis adopts removes matching altogether: score the teacher's
predictions under the student's \emph{full mixture density} $p_S$
(Section~\ref{ssec:method_pi}). This is permutation invariant by construction
(Proposition~\ref{prop:perm}), needs no assignment, is differentiable everywhere,
and is defined for any $K_S,K_T$. Two design choices remain inside this family. The
first is \emph{what} of each teacher mode to score: its mean (variant v1,
Section~\ref{ssec:method_v1}) or samples carrying its spread (variant v2,
Section~\ref{ssec:method_v2}). The second is the \emph{direction} of the
Kullback--Leibler divergence between the two mixtures: the \emph{forward}
$\mathrm{KL}(p_T\Vert p_S)$, which is \emph{mode-covering} (it forces the student to
put mass on every manoeuvre the teacher finds plausible), versus the \emph{reverse}
$\mathrm{KL}(p_S\Vert p_T)$, which is \emph{mode-seeking} (it lets the student lock
onto a subset of the teacher's modes and ignore the rest). The proposed objective is the
forward direction; Section~\ref{ssec:method_v2} shows v2 \emph{is} the (Monte-Carlo
estimated) forward KL and argues that mode-covering is the safety-correct choice for
trajectory prediction---a student must never drop a manoeuvre the teacher considers
possible.

\subsection{A Permutation-Invariant Mixture Objective}
\label{ssec:method_pi}
The proposed solution is to stop matching modes by index and instead score the
teacher's predictions under the student's \emph{full mixture density}. Let the
student predict the mixture $p_S$ of~\eqref{eq:mixture} with $K_S$ modes
$\{(\pi^S_k,\loc^S_k,\scale^S_k)\}$, and let the (frozen) teacher provide $K_T$
modes $\{(\pi^T_f,\loc^T_f,\scale^T_f)\}$. The distillation objective treats the
teacher's modes as a \emph{confidence-weighted set of soft targets} and evaluates
each under $p_S$, weighting by the teacher's confidence $\pi^T_f$. This single
design choice yields the invariance we need.

We now derive this objective explicitly, building from a single coordinate up to
its closed form. Each teacher mode is scored at its predicted trajectory (mean)
$\loc^T_f$, with the teacher's spread $\scale^T_f$ set aside for now; this choice
yields the first variant of the loss (v1), and revisiting \emph{what} is scored in
its place gives the second (v2, Section~\ref{ssec:method_v2}). The derivation
proceeds in four steps---one coordinate, one trajectory, the student mixture, and
the teacher-weighted sum---each collapsing one axis of the computation.

\paragraph{Per-element density.} For one agent, a student mode $k$, and a teacher
mode $f$. At prediction step $t$ and coordinate $d\in\{1,2\}$ the student's mode
$k$ places a one-dimensional Laplace bump centred at $\loc^S_{k,t,d}$ with scale
$\scale^S_{k,t,d}$. From the Laplace NLL~\eqref{eq:laplace_nll}, the log-density it
assigns to the teacher's predicted coordinate $\loc^T_{f,t,d}$ is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:per_elem}
  \log\Lap\!\left(\loc^T_{f,t,d}\mid \loc^S_{k,t,d},\scale^S_{k,t,d}\right)
    = -\log\!\big(2\,\scale^S_{k,t,d}\big)
      - \frac{\bigl\lvert \loc^T_{f,t,d}-\loc^S_{k,t,d}\bigr\rvert}{\scale^S_{k,t,d}}.
\end{equation}
This is largest when the student's prediction coincides with the teacher's at that
step and coordinate, and decreases linearly with their separation, measured in
units of the student's own scale.

\paragraph{From elements to a trajectory.} HiVT emits each mode as a
\emph{factorised} Laplace ( a whole trajectory is built as a product of 
independent 1-D Laplace densities), so the log-likelihood the student's mode $k$ assigns to
the whole teacher trajectory $\loc^T_f$ is the sum of~\eqref{eq:per_elem} over the
$H$ steps and the two coordinates,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:traj_score}
  \ell_{k,f}
    \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{H}\sum_{d=1}^{2}
          \log\Lap\!\left(\loc^T_{f,t,d}\mid \loc^S_{k,t,d},\scale^S_{k,t,d}\right),
\end{equation}
a sum of $2H$ per-element terms ($2\times30$ on Argoverse). Summing in log-space is
equivalent to multiplying the densities, so $\ell_{k,f}$ is the log of the joint
likelihood of the entire teacher path under student mode $k$. The factorisation
in~\eqref{eq:traj_score} is precisely the \emph{conditional}-independence
assumption already built into HiVT's mixture head~\eqref{eq:mixture}. It does
\emph{not} assert that successive positions are physically independent---they are
strongly correlated, since $x_{t+1}$ follows from $x_t$---but this correlation
is carried by the mode itself (each $\loc^S_k$ is one coherent, fully correlated
trajectory) and by the mixture over modes, rather than by an explicit covariance
across steps. The distillation loss inherits the same factorisation under which the
model is trained and evaluated. Evaluating $\ell_{k,f}$ over every pair yields a
$K_S\times K_T$ array of trajectory-to-trajectory scores (here $6\times6=36$).

\paragraph{From a mode to the mixture.} A teacher trajectory should be scored under
the student's \emph{entire} predictive distribution, not a single component. We
therefore add each student mode's log-weight $\log\pi^S_k$---discounting modes the
student itself deems unlikely---and marginalise over $k$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mix_score}
  \log p_S\!\left(\loc^T_f\right)
    \;=\; \log\sum_{k=1}^{K_S}\exp\!\big(\log\pi^S_k + \ell_{k,f}\big)
    \;=\; \log\sum_{k=1}^{K_S}\pi^S_k\,
          \Lap\!\left(\loc^T_f\mid\loc^S_k,\scale^S_k\right).
\end{equation}
The inner sum is the mixture density~\eqref{eq:mixture} evaluated at the teacher
point; in practice it is computed with a numerically stable log-sum-exp to avoid
underflow when the target lands far from every student mode. Two properties of this
step matter. First, the output is a \emph{single} scalar per teacher mode---the
mixture log-likelihood of $\loc^T_f$---and \emph{not} a distribution over the
student's $K_S$ modes; such a distribution would be the posterior responsibilities
$\pi^S_k\Lap/\sum_j\pi^S_j\Lap$, which the objective never forms. Second, the
log-sum-exp acts as a \emph{smooth maximum}: it is dominated by whichever student
mode best explains $\loc^T_f$ yet stays differentiable in all of them. This is
where the absence of pairing materialises---teacher mode $f$ is explained by
whichever student mode(s) happen to lie near it, selected by their values rather
than by a shared index.

\paragraph{Assembling the objective.} Finally, each teacher mode is weighted by the
teacher's own confidence $\pi^T_f$ and the contributions are summed, so that modes
the teacher considers likely dominate the target and modes it nearly ignores
contribute little. Substituting the mixture score~\eqref{eq:mix_score} into this
confidence-weighted sum gives the mean-target distillation loss in closed form,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:v1}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v1}}
    \;=\; -\sum_{f=1}^{K_T} \pi^T_f \, \log p_S\!\left(\loc^T_f\right)
    \;=\; -\sum_{f=1}^{K_T} \pi^T_f \,
        \log \sum_{k=1}^{K_S} \pi^S_k \,
        \Lap\!\left(\loc^T_f \mid \loc^S_k,\scale^S_k\right).
\end{equation}
The two nested sums are exactly the two collapses of the construction: the inner
$\log\sum_k$ is the mixture score~\eqref{eq:mix_score} that marginalises the
student's modes, and each $\Lap(\loc^T_f\mid\loc^S_k,\scale^S_k)$ is the factorised
product of the per-step densities~\eqref{eq:per_elem} over the $H$ steps and two
coordinates; the outer $\sum_f$ is the confidence-weighted average over the
teacher's modes. Equation~\eqref{eq:v1} is the per-agent loss; the training
objective averages it over the agents in a batch, with a constant per-element factor
$1/(2H)$ that rescales it onto the scale of HiVT's regression term so the
distillation weight $\lambda$ is easy to set---being constant, it leaves the optimum
unchanged.
\begin{prop}[Permutation invariance]
\label{prop:perm}
Consider any objective of the form
$\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{f=1}^{K_T} \pi^T_f\, g\!\left(p_S, \loc^T_f, \scale^T_f\right)$,
in which the student enters \emph{only} through its mixture density $p_S$ (and
never through an individual student mode). Then reordering the student's modes or
the teacher's modes leaves $\mathcal{L}$ unchanged, and $\mathcal{L}$ refers to no
correspondence between the two index sets---so it needs no matching and is defined
for any $K_S$ and $K_T$, including $K_S \neq K_T$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
Both index sets enter only through sums, and a finite sum does not depend on the
order of its terms. The student appears only inside
$p_S(\cdot)=\sum_{k}\pi^S_k\Lap(\cdot\mid\loc^S_k,\scale^S_k)$, so permuting the
student modes leaves $p_S$---and hence $\mathcal{L}$---unchanged. The teacher
appears only inside the outer sum $\sum_f$, so permuting the teacher modes leaves
$\mathcal{L}$ unchanged as well. Because neither sum pairs a student index with a
teacher index, no matching is ever invoked and the objective is defined for any
$K_S,K_T$.
\end{proof}

The two distillation losses are both instances of this form; they differ only in the
choice of $g$, i.e.\ in \emph{what} of each teacher mode is scored under $p_S$: v1,
just derived in~\eqref{eq:v1}, scores the mean, while v2
(Section~\ref{ssec:method_v2}) scores samples that also carry the teacher's scale.
Invariance is a \emph{necessary} property here, not a sufficient one: it guarantees
the loss never compares mismatched slots, but says nothing about whether the
resulting target is a good one---indeed v1 is permutation invariant yet still
degrades calibration (\S\ref{ssec:method_calib}).

\subsection{v1: Mean-Target Distillation}
\label{ssec:method_v1}
The objective derived above, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v1}}$ in~\eqref{eq:v1}, is the
first variant: it treats each teacher mode as a single \emph{point} target---its
predicted trajectory (mean) $\loc^T_f$, with the teacher's spread discarded---and
scores that point under the student mixture density. We now read off its meaning and
its consequences.

Read as an objective, \eqref{eq:v1} is a confidence-weighted cross-entropy: it takes
the teacher's mode means as a set of soft targets, weighted by the teacher's own
confidence $\pi^T_f$, and maximises the student mixture's log-likelihood of them. It
thus transfers \emph{where} the teacher expects the agent to go (the means
$\loc^T_f$) and \emph{how likely} it considers each (the weights $\pi^T_f$), while
\emph{discarding the teacher's scales} $\scale^T_f$---which appear nowhere on the
right-hand side. Equivalently, each teacher mode is treated as a Dirac point mass at
$\loc^T_f$ rather than as the spread-out Laplace the teacher actually predicted; this
is exactly the zero-variance case from which v2 departs
(Proposition~\ref{prop:limit}).

By Proposition~\ref{prop:perm} the objective is permutation invariant: the student
enters only through its mixture density $p_S$ and the teacher only through the outer
$\pi^T_f$-weighted sum, so neither model's mode ordering matters. As shown next, this
objective improves best-of-$K$ geometry---the student's mode locations are pulled
toward the teacher's accurate ones---but systematically degrades calibration, a
direct consequence of those discarded scales.


\subsection{Why Mean-Target Distillation Breaks Calibration}
\label{ssec:method_calib}
The failure is a direct consequence of discarding $\scale^T_f$, and it can be read
off the Laplace NLL. Consider, for intuition, a single student mode whose location
already sits at a target $\mu$; its contribution to~\eqref{eq:v1} as a function of
its own scale $b$ is, from~\eqref{eq:laplace_nll} with $y=\mu$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:shrink}
  -\log\Lap(\mu\mid\mu,b) = \log(2b),
\end{equation}
which is \emph{monotonically increasing in $b$} and minimised as $b\to 0$. Scoring a
point target thus rewards arbitrarily small scales: nothing in $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v1}}$
opposes shrinkage. Contrast the baseline regression loss~\eqref{eq:laplace_nll},
where the residual term $|y-\mu|/b$ \emph{grows} as $b\to0$ whenever the prediction
is imperfect, so the two terms balance at a finite optimum.

\begin{remark}[Optimal scale of the Laplace NLL]
\label{prop:optb}
For a fixed location $\mu$ and target drawn from a distribution with
$\mathbb{E}\,|y-\mu| < \infty$, the scale minimising the expected Laplace NLL
$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log(2b) + |y-\mu|/b\right]$ is $b^\star = \mathbb{E}\,|y-\mu|$:
differentiating $\phi(b)=\log(2b) + \mathbb{E}|y-\mu|/b$ gives
$\phi'(b)=\tfrac{1}{b}-\tfrac{\mathbb{E}|y-\mu|}{b^2}$, which vanishes at
$b=\mathbb{E}|y-\mu|$, and $\phi''(b^\star)=1/{b^\star}^2>0$ confirms it is the
unique minimiser. This is the standard Laplace maximum-likelihood scale (the mean
absolute deviation).
\end{remark}

Remark~\ref{prop:optb} says the well-trained baseline learns a scale that
\emph{reports its own typical error}: $b$ is a calibrated uncertainty. The
mean-target loss removes the residual term that anchors this balance, so the
student's scales are driven below their calibrated value and the predicted
intervals become too narrow---the student is \emph{over-confident}.

The single-mode argument above is an intuition; the same conclusion holds for the
\emph{full} mixture objective~\eqref{eq:v1}, with the mixture's responsibilities
making it precise. For a student mode $k$, step $t$ and coordinate $d$, define the
\emph{responsibility} of mode $k$ for teacher mean $f$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:resp}
  r_{f\to k}
    \;=\; \frac{\pi^S_k\,\Lap\!\left(\loc^T_f\mid\loc^S_k,\scale^S_k\right)}
               {\sum_{j}\pi^S_j\,\Lap\!\left(\loc^T_f\mid\loc^S_j,\scale^S_j\right)}
    \;=\; \frac{\pi^S_k\,\Lap\!\left(\loc^T_f\mid\loc^S_k,\scale^S_k\right)}{p_S(\loc^T_f)},
\end{equation}
the posterior probability, under the student mixture, that the teacher point
$\loc^T_f$ was generated by student mode $k$.
\begin{prop}[Responsibility-weighted stationary scale under v1]
\label{prop:v1grad}
The gradient of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v1}}$ in~\eqref{eq:v1} with respect to a
student scale $\scale^S_{k,t,d}$ is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:v1grad}
  \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v1}}}{\partial \scale^S_{k,t,d}}
    \;=\; \sum_{f=1}^{K_T} \pi^T_f\, r_{f\to k}
      \left(\frac{1}{\scale^S_{k,t,d}}
            - \frac{\bigl\lvert \loc^T_{f,t,d}-\loc^S_{k,t,d}\bigr\rvert}
                   {\bigl(\scale^S_{k,t,d}\bigr)^2}\right),
\end{equation}
so a stationary scale satisfies the responsibility-weighted fixed-point condition
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:v1bstar}
  \scale^{S\star}_{k,t,d}
    \;=\; \frac{\sum_f \pi^T_f\, r_{f\to k}\,
                \bigl\lvert \loc^T_{f,t,d}-\loc^S_{k,t,d}\bigr\rvert}
               {\sum_f \pi^T_f\, r_{f\to k}} .
\end{equation}
Because the responsibilities $r_{f\to k}$ themselves depend on
$\scale^S_{k,t,d}$ (through both the numerator $\Lap$ and $p_S$ in~\eqref{eq:resp}),
Equation~\eqref{eq:v1bstar} is an implicit self-consistency condition that
\emph{characterises} the stationary scale rather than an explicit closed-form
minimiser; the shrinkage conclusion below does not depend on solving it.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
Write $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v1}}=-\sum_f \pi^T_f \log p_S(\loc^T_f)$. Since
$p_S(\loc^T_f)=\sum_j \pi^S_j\Lap(\loc^T_f\mid\loc^S_j,\scale^S_j)$ and only the
$j{=}k$ term depends on $\scale^S_{k}$,
$\partial \log p_S(\loc^T_f)/\partial \scale^S_{k,t,d}
 = r_{f\to k}\,\partial \log\Lap(\loc^T_{f,t,d}\mid\loc^S_{k,t,d},\scale^S_{k,t,d})/\partial\scale^S_{k,t,d}$
by~\eqref{eq:resp}. From the Laplace NLL~\eqref{eq:laplace_nll},
$\partial[-\log(2\scale)-|y-\mu|/\scale]/\partial\scale = -1/\scale + |y-\mu|/\scale^2$.
Substituting and negating gives~\eqref{eq:v1grad}; setting it to zero and solving
for $\scale^S_{k,t,d}$ gives~\eqref{eq:v1bstar}.
\end{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:v1grad} contains \emph{no term that opposes shrinkage}: every
contribution pushes $\scale^S_{k,t,d}$ toward the residual $|\loc^T_f-\loc^S_k|$,
and there is no penalty for $\scale\to0$ beyond it. This is the crux. The location
part of v1 \emph{actively} drives each student mode onto the teacher means it is
responsible for, so those residuals shrink toward zero; by~\eqref{eq:v1bstar} the
stationary scale shrinks with them, $\scale^{S\star}\to0$, and the student collapses
to a spike at the teacher point. The fix in v2 is now visible in advance: replacing
the point target $\loc^T_f$ by samples $\mathbf{y}\sim\Lap(\loc^T_f,\scale^T_f)$
makes the residual $\mathbb{E}\,|\mathbf{y}-\loc^S_k|$ bounded below by the
teacher's own scale even at a perfect location match, so~\eqref{eq:v1bstar} settles
at $\scale^{S\star}\!\approx\!\scale^T_f>0$ rather than $0$
(Section~\ref{ssec:method_v2}).

Crucially this is a per-mode \emph{scale} pathology, not a collapse of the mode
\emph{weights}: the argument touches only $\scale$, leaving $\{\pi_k\}$ untouched.
The diagnostics in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} confirm exactly this signature---scales
shrink while mode-weight entropy is unchanged.

\subsection{v2: Distribution-Matching Distillation}
\label{ssec:method_v2}
The fix is to restore the discarded spread by scoring not the bare location but
\emph{samples drawn from the teacher mode using its own scale} $\scale^T_f$. Define
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:v2}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v2}}
    = -\sum_{f=1}^{K_T} \pi^T_f \,
       \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim \Lap(\loc^T_f,\,\scale^T_f)}
        \!\left[\log p_S(\mathbf{y})\right],
\end{equation}
estimated by drawing $S$ Monte-Carlo samples per teacher mode and averaging. The
student must now place density across the \emph{whole region the teacher considers
plausible}, not merely at its centre, so collapsing its scales no longer minimises
the loss: a too-narrow student misses the teacher's outer samples and is penalised.
The objective is again of the form in Proposition~\ref{prop:perm}, hence
permutation invariant, and it relates cleanly to v1.

\begin{prop}[v2 generalises v1]
\label{prop:limit}
As the teacher scales vanish, $\scale^T_f\to\mathbf{0}$, the distribution-matching
loss~\eqref{eq:v2} reduces to the mean-target loss~\eqref{eq:v1}.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
As $\scale^T_f\to\mathbf{0}$ the Laplace $\Lap(\loc^T_f,\scale^T_f)$ converges to the
Dirac measure $\delta_{\loc^T_f}$, so
$\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim\Lap(\loc^T_f,\scale^T_f)}[\log p_S(\mathbf{y})]
\to \log p_S(\loc^T_f)$. Substituting into~\eqref{eq:v2} gives~\eqref{eq:v1}.
\end{proof}

Thus v2 is the strict generalisation that v1 is the zero-variance limit of: it
transfers the teacher's full predictive distribution---locations, weights,
\emph{and} scales---and recovers v1 exactly when the teacher is certain.

\paragraph{v2 is the forward KL from teacher to student.} Collecting the teacher's
modes into a single mixture density
$p_T(\mathbf{y})=\sum_f \pi^T_f\,\Lap(\mathbf{y}\mid\loc^T_f,\scale^T_f)$, the
$\pi^T_f$-weighted expectation in~\eqref{eq:v2} is precisely an expectation under
$p_T$, so
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:v2kl}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v2}}
    = -\sum_{f} \pi^T_f\,
       \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim\Lap(\loc^T_f,\scale^T_f)}\!\left[\log p_S(\mathbf{y})\right]
    = -\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim p_T}\!\left[\log p_S(\mathbf{y})\right]
    = \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_T \Vert p_S\right)}_{\text{depends on }S}
      + \underbrace{H(p_T)}_{\text{constant}} .
\end{equation}
The cross-entropy $-\mathbb{E}_{p_T}\!\log p_S$ decomposes into the \emph{forward}
KL plus the teacher's own entropy $H(p_T)$, and the latter does not depend on the
student. Hence $\arg\min_S \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v2}} = \arg\min_S
\mathrm{KL}(p_T\Vert p_S)$: minimising v2 is exactly minimising the forward KL from
the teacher mixture to the student mixture, estimated by the $S$ Monte-Carlo draws.
This gives the method a clean information-theoretic identity---v2 is
distribution matching in the strict sense---and locates v1 within it: v1 is the
same cross-entropy with $p_T$'s per-mode spread collapsed to point masses
(Proposition~\ref{prop:limit}), which is why it matches the teacher's mode
\emph{locations} but discards its \emph{spread} and so breaks calibration
(\S\ref{ssec:method_calib}).

\paragraph{Why the forward, mode-covering direction.} The forward KL
$\mathrm{KL}(p_T\Vert p_S)$ is \emph{mode-covering} (zero-avoiding): its integrand
$p_T(\mathbf{y})\log\!\big(p_T(\mathbf{y})/p_S(\mathbf{y})\big)$ diverges wherever
the teacher places mass but the student does not ($p_S\!\to\!0$ while $p_T\!>\!0$),
so the student is driven to put density on \emph{every} manoeuvre the teacher deems
plausible. The reverse KL $\mathrm{KL}(p_S\Vert p_T)$ is instead
\emph{mode-seeking} (zero-forcing): it permits the student to lock onto a single
teacher mode and ignore the rest. For trajectory prediction this asymmetry is
decisive on safety grounds---\emph{dropping} a plausible manoeuvre (failing to
predict that the agent might turn) is the dangerous error, since a downstream
planner that never receives a hypothesis cannot prepare for it, whereas an extra
low-probability mode is merely conservative. Mode-covering is therefore the correct
direction, and v2 implements it by construction. Forward KL is additionally the
simpler direction to optimise against the offline teacher cache: because the
expectation is taken under the \emph{frozen} teacher, the samples can be drawn
once from the cached mixture parameters $\{\pi^T_f,\mu^T_f,b^T_f\}$ and treated as
constants, so the gradient flows through $\log p_S$ alone with no reparameterisation
(Section~\ref{ssec:method_impl}). Reverse KL is equally implementable from the same
cache---the Laplace-mixture density $p_T(\mathbf{y})$ and its score are closed-form
at any query point, so they can be evaluated at fresh student draws without a teacher
forward pass---but it requires reparameterised samples from the \emph{moving} student
at every step, a simplicity/efficiency cost rather than a feasibility barrier.

  \caption[Forward vs.\ reverse KL: mode-covering vs.\ mode-seeking.]{The two KL

\subsection{The Combined Objective}
\label{ssec:method_total}
In both cases the distillation term is added to HiVT's original loss with a weight
$\lambda$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:total}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HiVT}} + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}},
  \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}\in\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v1}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v2}}\},
\end{equation}
so the student is supervised by the ground truth and the teacher simultaneously. We
note that $\lambda$ is \emph{not directly comparable across v1 and v2}: because
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v2}}$ averages a log-density over $S$ samples while
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v1}}$ scores a single point, the two terms have different
magnitudes at the same nominal $\lambda$, so an identical $\lambda$ corresponds to a
different effective distillation strength in each. The weight is therefore tuned
separately per variant (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{ssec:method_impl}
Three implementation concerns determine whether the distillation signal actually
reaches the student.

\paragraph{Offline teacher caching.}
The teacher is frozen, so its predictions for every training scene are computed
\emph{once} and cached to disk (an HDF5 store of per-scene mode parameters
$\{\pi^T_f,\loc^T_f,\scale^T_f\}$). Training then reads the teacher targets from the
cache rather than running a teacher forward pass each step, making the per-epoch
teacher cost effectively zero and decoupling student training speed from teacher
size.

\paragraph{Focal-agent target alignment.}
HiVT predicts all agents in a scene but is evaluated and supervised on the focal
agent. The cached teacher targets must be aligned to the same focal agent and the
same local reference frame as the student's predictions before the loss is formed;
a misalignment here silently distills against the wrong agent. The pipeline asserts
this correspondence explicitly.

\paragraph{Numerical stability and diagnostics.}
The mixture log-density $\log\sum_k \pi^S_k\,\Lap(\cdot)$ is evaluated in
log-space with a $\lse$ reduction to avoid underflow when component densities are
small. Because distillation can fail \emph{silently}---a mis-scaled or
mis-aligned signal still produces a plausible loss curve---training is instrumented
with diagnostics that surface such failures: predicted sharpness ($b$-scale),
reliability/coverage, mode-weight entropy, and offline mode diversity, reported in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\paragraph{The loss in full.}
Algorithms~\ref{alg:v1} and~\ref{alg:v2} state the per-agent computation of the two
variants end to end, taking the cached teacher parameters and the student mixture as
inputs and returning the scalar of~\eqref{eq:v1} or~\eqref{eq:v2}. Both share the
same skeleton---accumulate per-element Laplace log-densities over the horizon, add
the student log-weights, reduce the student modes with a stable $\lse$, then take the
teacher-confidence-weighted sum---and differ only in v2's inner Monte-Carlo loop,
which replaces each teacher mean by $S$ reparameterised Laplace samples
(line~\ref{algline:sample}) that carry the teacher's scale $\scale^T_f$.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Mean-target distillation loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v1}}$ (per agent)}
\label{alg:v1}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require teacher cache $\{\pi^T_f,\loc^T_f\}_{f=1}^{K_T}$; student mixture
  $\{z^S_k,\loc^S_k,\scale^S_k\}_{k=1}^{K_S}$ ($z^S$: mode logits); horizon $H$
\State $\log\pi^S \gets \textsc{LogSoftmax}(z^S)$;\quad
       $\pi^T \gets \textsc{Softmax}(z^T)$
\For{$f = 1$ \textbf{to} $K_T$}
  \For{$k = 1$ \textbf{to} $K_S$}
    \State $\ell_{k} \gets \sum_{t=1}^{H}\sum_{d=1}^{2}
            \big[-\log(2\scale^S_{k,t,d}) - |\loc^T_{f,t,d}-\loc^S_{k,t,d}|/\scale^S_{k,t,d}\big]$
  \EndFor
  \State $L_f \gets \lse_{k}\big(\log\pi^S_k + \ell_{k}\big)$
         \Comment{$=\log p_S(\loc^T_f)$, stable log-sum-exp}
\EndFor
\State \Return $-\dfrac{1}{2H}\sum_{f=1}^{K_T}\pi^T_f\,L_f$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Distribution-matching distillation loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v2}}$ (per agent)}
\label{alg:v2}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require teacher cache $\{\pi^T_f,\loc^T_f,\scale^T_f\}_{f=1}^{K_T}$; student mixture
  $\{z^S_k,\loc^S_k,\scale^S_k\}_{k=1}^{K_S}$; samples $S$; horizon $H$
\State $\log\pi^S \gets \textsc{LogSoftmax}(z^S)$;\quad
       $\pi^T \gets \textsc{Softmax}(z^T)$
\For{$f = 1$ \textbf{to} $K_T$}
  \State $A_f \gets 0$
  \For{$s = 1$ \textbf{to} $S$}
    \State draw $u\sim\mathcal{U}(-\tfrac12,\tfrac12)^{2H}$;\quad
           $\mathbf{y} \gets \loc^T_f - \scale^T_f\odot\operatorname{sgn}(u)\odot\log(1-2|u|)$
           \label{algline:sample}
           \Comment{reparam.\ Laplace}
    \For{$k = 1$ \textbf{to} $K_S$}
      \State $\ell_{k} \gets \sum_{t,d}
              \big[-\log(2\scale^S_{k,t,d}) - |\mathbf{y}_{t,d}-\loc^S_{k,t,d}|/\scale^S_{k,t,d}\big]$
    \EndFor
    \State $A_f \gets A_f + \lse_{k}\big(\log\pi^S_k + \ell_{k}\big)$
           \Comment{$=\log p_S(\mathbf{y})$}
  \EndFor
  \State $L_f \gets A_f / S$ \Comment{Monte-Carlo estimate of $\mathbb{E}\,\log p_S$}
\EndFor
\State \Return $-\dfrac{1}{2H}\sum_{f=1}^{K_T}\pi^T_f\,L_f$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Cost.}
Per agent, v1 evaluates the $K_S\times K_T$ trajectory scores at $O(K_T K_S H)$ and
v2 multiplies this by the sample count, $O(K_T\,S\,K_S\,H)$; both are linear in
every factor and free of any assignment solve. This compares favourably with the
$O(K^3)$ Hungarian matching of the alternative in
Section~\ref{ssec:method_design}, and---unlike a matching loss---is differentiable
and needs no special handling when $K_S\neq K_T$. The teacher's own cost is
\emph{amortised to zero}: its mixture parameters are read from the offline cache in
$O(K_T H)$ per scene rather than recomputed, so the distillation overhead is
independent of teacher size. With the Argoverse settings
($K_S{=}K_T{=}6$, $S{=}8$, $H{=}30$) the loss adds a few thousand floating-point
operations per agent, negligible beside the student forward pass.
