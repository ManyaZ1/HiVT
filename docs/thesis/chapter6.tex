\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the experimental results. It first establishes the
from-scratch capacity baselines that quantify the accuracy cost of shrinking HiVT
(Section~\ref{sec:res_baselines}) and the recipe choices that make those baselines
honest (Section~\ref{sec:res_recipe}). It then presents the central result---the
contrast between the two distillation objectives on HiVT-32
(Section~\ref{sec:res_kd32})---and its replication, with a larger relative gain, on
the smaller HiVT-16, where the gain is further confirmed across independent random
seeds (Section~\ref{sec:res_kd16}). Finally it consolidates the
accuracy--capacity trade-off across all models (Section~\ref{sec:res_frontier}),
measures the inference efficiency that motivates the whole exercise
(Section~\ref{sec:res_eff}), and closes with qualitative and diagnostic analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:res_qual}). Unless noted otherwise, every number is computed by
\texttt{eval.py} on the full Argoverse~1 validation split, and the primary metric
is minFDE$_6$.

\section{From-Scratch Capacity Baselines}
\label{sec:res_baselines}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} reports the four HiVT widths trained (or, for the
authors' released checkpoints, evaluated) without any distillation. They span a
$55\times$ range in parameters and form the reference frame for every later claim:
the distillation gain is always measured as movement \emph{relative to the
same-width from-scratch model}.

    $2$ attention heads (Section~\ref{ssec:res_heads}).}

    \bottomrule

Two patterns matter for what follows. First, geometric error grows
\emph{faster than linearly} as width falls: halving width from $64$ to $32$ costs
$+0.127$\,m minFDE, but halving again from $32$ to $16$ costs $+0.448$\,m---the
accuracy penalty steepens with every halving, so it is precisely the smallest
students that have the most for distillation to recover. (Width and parameter count
are themselves not linearly related: the parameter count scales roughly with $d^2$,
so each halving of the width removes about three-quarters of the parameters---as the
Params column makes plain ($170{,}073\!\to\!45{,}929$ from $d{=}32$ to $d{=}16$) and
as developed in Section~\ref{sec:res_eff}---which is why the steep accuracy loss at
the bottom of the ladder is paired with a disproportionately large parameter saving.)
Second, the from-scratch models are already
\emph{well calibrated} (calibration error $\le 0.047$ everywhere, in fact lowest at
the smallest widths): calibration is not a pre-existing defect to be fixed, which
is precisely why a distillation method that \emph{damages} it (v1,
Section~\ref{sec:res_kd32}) is unacceptable even when it improves geometry.

\section{Student Recipe Selection}
\label{sec:res_recipe}

The students trained in this work require two width-dependent choices---learning
rate and attention-head count---that were fixed by short sweeps before any
distillation. Settling these on the \emph{from-scratch} model ensures the later
distillation comparison is against a properly tuned, not a handicapped, baseline.
The throughput-only settings---batch size, data-loader workers, and
\texttt{bfloat16} mixed precision---are not width-dependent, were tuned once for the
training machine (an RTX~5060), and are held fixed across every run reported in this
chapter; they affect wall-clock time but not accuracy and are detailed in
Section~\ref{ssec:setup_models}.

\subsection{Learning rate}
\label{ssec:res_lr}
HiVT-32 trains best at $\eta=3\times10^{-3}$, selected by a learning-rate sweep on a
$25\%$-data proxy (Table~\ref{tab:lr32}). Across the five rates swept, geometry
improves as $\eta$ rises from $5\times10^{-4}$ to $3\times10^{-3}$---the smallest
rate underfits badly in the short schedule (minFDE $2.54$)---and then \emph{regresses}
at $4\times10^{-3}$, giving a clean interior optimum at $\eta=3\times10^{-3}$, best on
every metric; Figure~\ref{fig:lr32} shows the same ranking in the convergence curves.
This rate is used for all HiVT-32 runs.

\subsection{Attention heads}
\label{ssec:res_heads}
HiVT uses $8$ attention heads by default, so the per-head dimension shrinks with
width (Section~\ref{ssec:setup_models}). At HiVT-32 this is benign:
Table~\ref{tab:heads32} compares $2$ heads (head dimension $16$) against the
default $8$ (head dimension $4$) on the from-scratch model, and the two are
indistinguishable on geometry---every displacement metric moves under $\sim\!2\%$,
i.e.\ single-run noise, and calibration differs by under $3\%$. At $d{=}32$ there is simply not
enough width for $8$ heads to express more than $2$, so head count is not a
meaningful lever and the baseline may use either; the default $8$ is retained.

    calib.\ err & 0.0316 & 0.0325 & $-2.9\%$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

HiVT-16 is the opposite case. The per-head dimension is $16/\mathrm{nh}$, so the
default $8$ heads leave only $2$ channels per head---too few for the attention to
be expressive. Table~\ref{tab:heads16} sweeps nh $\in\{2,4,8\}$ on the $25\%$
proxy: geometry degrades \emph{monotonically} as the head dimension shrinks, with
minFDE rising from $2.95$ (nh$=2$, head dimension $8$) through $3.25$ (nh$=4$) to
$3.69$ (nh$=8$, head dimension $2$)---a $25\%$ relative loss---and the best-mode
regression NLL nearly doubling at nh$=8$, a sign the smallest-head model trains
poorly. (This nh$=8$ entry is a \emph{separate} run from the also-$8$-head
$\eta=1\times10^{-3}$ column of the learning-rate sweep in Table~\ref{tab:lr16},
which reached $2.91$: the same $8$-head configuration lands at $2.91$ in one run and
$3.69$ in the other because, with only two channels per head, the $8$-head model
trains \emph{unstably} and sometimes settles in the poor ${\approx}3.2$--$3.7$ basin
of Section~\ref{ssec:res_seeds}. The $2$-head model shows no such run-to-run spread,
a further reason to prefer it.) Two heads is clearly best, so HiVT-16 uses $2$ heads
for every reported result; Figure~\ref{fig:heads16} shows the same monotone degradation in the
convergence curves. This mirrors and sharpens the HiVT-32 finding: head count is immaterial
when the width is ample (HiVT-32) but becomes a real lever once the width is small
enough that adding heads starves each of dimension (HiVT-16).

\section{Distillation on HiVT-32: Mean-Target vs.\ Distribution-Matching}
\label{sec:res_kd32}

This section is the core empirical result. It shows, on HiVT-32, that the naive
mean-target objective (v1) improves geometry but \emph{collapses calibration},
that the failure is the per-mode scale shrinkage predicted in
Section~\ref{ssec:method_calib}, and that the distribution-matching objective (v2)
retains the full geometric gain while repairing calibration.

\subsection{The distillation-weight sweep}
\label{ssec:res_sweep32}
Figure~\ref{fig:lambda32} sweeps the distillation weight $\lambda$ for v1 on a
$25\%$-data proxy. The five panels tell the whole v1 story at once: geometry
(minFDE) improves sharply and then saturates, while every calibration diagnostic
moves the wrong way---mixNLL turns up, the mean Laplace scale $b$ \emph{shrinks}
monotonically, and the calibration error climbs steeply. Crucially the mode-weight
entropy is \emph{flat} across $\lambda$: the damage is not a collapse of the
mixture weights but a sharpening of each mode's predicted spread, exactly the
signature derived analytically in Section~\ref{ssec:method_calib}. The proxy ranks
$\lambda\!\approx\!0.25$--$0.5$ best on geometry; $\lambda=0.5$ is carried forward
as the operating point, being the standard value in the distillation literature
and a reasonable geometry/calibration compromise.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/kd_lambda_sweep.png}
  \caption{HiVT-32 v1 (mean-target) distillation-weight sweep ($25\%$ data).
    Geometry (minFDE, left) improves with $\lambda$, but mixNLL, calibration
    error rise and the mean Laplace scale $b$ shrinks, while mode-weight entropy
    (right) is unchanged. The pathology is scale shrinkage, not mode collapse.}
  \label{fig:lambda32}

\subsection{v1: accuracy up, calibration down}
\label{ssec:res_v1}
Table~\ref{tab:ab32} is the clean A/B at full data and the chosen $\lambda=0.5$:
the same recipe, with the distillation term switched on. Geometry improves
substantially and consistently---minFDE $-9.2\%$, MR $-13.4\%$, minADE
$-5.5\%$---and the official ranking metric brier-minFDE improves by $5.2\%$. But
the full-distribution metrics collapse: mixNLL worsens by $43\%$ and the
calibration error rises more than five-fold, from $0.033$ to $0.184$. The mean scale $b$
drops $36\%$ ($0.418\to0.268$): the student has been driven well below its own
calibrated width and is now systematically over-confident, as the reliability
curves of Figure~\ref{fig:reliability}(b) confirm---the predicted intervals cover far
fewer ground-truth points than their nominal probability, and the under-coverage
worsens monotonically with $\lambda$. The same shrinkage shows in the two final
rows: the predicted $90\%$ interval now captures only $71.1\%$ of ground-truth
endpoints (cov@p90 $0.903\to0.711$), and even HiVT's own winner-takes-all
regression NLL (reg-loss) worsens ($-0.247\to-0.187$)---the single \emph{best} mode
assigns lower density to the truth despite sitting geometrically closer to it,
because its scale has shrunk faster than its location error has fallen. The
over-confidence is thus visible even on the best mode, not only across the full
mixture.

\paragraph{How to read the reliability curves.}
The calibration metrics summarise the predicted \emph{uncertainty}, so it is worth
stating how they are formed. For the best mode's per-coordinate Laplace$(\mu,b)$,
the probability that the truth falls within a symmetric interval of half-width $t$
about $\mu$ is $P(|y-\mu|\le t)=1-e^{-t/b}$, so the central interval with nominal
coverage $p$ has half-width $t(p)=-b\ln(1-p)$. For each nominal level
$p\in\{0.1,\dots,0.9\}$ the \emph{empirical} coverage is the fraction of valid
(agent, step, coordinate) triples whose ground truth lies inside that interval;
plotting empirical against nominal gives the reliability curve, and a perfectly
calibrated model lies on the diagonal $\mathrm{empirical}(p)=p$. A curve \emph{below}
the diagonal means the intervals are too narrow---the truth escapes them more often
than promised---i.e.\ over-confidence; \emph{above} means under-confidence. The
single calibration-error scalar is the mean absolute gap
$\tfrac1L\sum_p|\mathrm{empirical}(p)-p|$ over the $L{=}9$ levels, and cov@p90 is the
empirical coverage at $p{=}0.9$.

This is the negative result that motivates v2, summarised as: \emph{mean-target
mixture distillation improves geometric accuracy and the official Argoverse ranking
metric but degrades full-distribution calibration, because it transfers the
teacher's mode locations and weights while discarding its predictive variance,
producing over-confident student mixtures.}

\subsection{v2: accuracy retained, calibration repaired}
\label{ssec:res_v2}

teacher's own scales, so collapsing the student's scales no longer minimises the
loss. Table~\ref{tab:v2_32} places v2 beside v1 and the baseline.

v2 ties v1 on geometry exactly (minFDE $1.0505$ vs.\ $1.0503$) and eliminates the
calibration penalty entirely: the calibration error falls to $0.0275$---$6.7\times$
better than v1, and lower than both the from-scratch baseline and the teacher---and
cov@p90 returns to nominal. The mean scale recovers to $0.413$, the student's own
calibrated width. (That this exceeds the teacher's $0.358$ is expected and correct:
a less-accurate student has larger typical error, so honest calibration
\emph{requires} a wider scale; v1's error was being sharper than even the teacher.)
Figure~\ref{fig:reliability}(b) shows the repair directly: the v2 reliability curve
sits on the from-scratch baseline at every nominal level, exactly where v1's bows
away. Panel~(a) shows that this is not a property of the chosen operating point---v2
stays on the diagonal across the whole $\lambda$ range over which v1 degrades
monotonically. v2 thus delivers the full accuracy gain of distillation at zero
calibration cost, confirming the chapter's central hypothesis on HiVT-32.

\section{Distillation on HiVT-16}
\label{sec:res_kd16}

The HiVT-16 experiments test whether the v2 result replicates at a smaller width
and whether, as hypothesised, the relative benefit \emph{grows} as the student
shrinks. Because the v1 calibration failure is already established, HiVT-16 is run
directly with v2. HiVT-16 is trained with $2$ attention heads and 
$\eta=1\times10^{-3}$ (Section~\ref{sec:res_recipe}).

\subsection{Distillation-weight sweep}
The $\lambda$-sweep ($25\%$ proxy, Table~\ref{tab:kl16}) shows a clear knee at
$\lambda=1.0$---higher than HiVT-32's optimum, consistent with a smaller student
benefiting from a stronger teacher signal. Geometry improves monotonically up to
$\lambda=1.0$ (minFDE $-16.4\%$ vs.\ no KD) and then regresses at $\lambda=2.0$.
Calibration stays intact throughout: mixNLL \emph{improves} with $\lambda$ and the
scale stays in the healthy band, with none of the v1 collapse. The $\lambda{=}0$
column here is an \emph{in-harness} no-KD anchor---the identical distillation code
path with the KD term switched off, on the final $2$-head model---so it is the
like-for-like baseline for the $\lambda{>}0$ columns. It is a \emph{separate} proxy
run from the learning-rate and head-count sweeps (Tables~\ref{tab:lr16},~\ref{tab:heads16},
whose no-KD anchors sit at ${\approx}2.9$ minFDE---the former on the un-tuned $8$-head
architecture), and reaches a slightly lower minFDE ($2.65$); the three $25\%$ proxies
are independent selection runs used only for \emph{within-table} ranking, so their
absolute anchors are not meant to coincide across tables.

\paragraph{Smaller students benefit more.}
The relative geometric gain from v2 is $-22.7\%$ minFDE at HiVT-16 against $-9.2\%$
at HiVT-32---roughly $2.5\times$ larger for the smaller network. This is the
expected ordering: the student with the largest gap to the teacher has the most to
gain from its guidance, and distillation is correspondingly more valuable exactly
where capacity is scarcest.
\subsection{Seed robustness}
\label{ssec:res_seeds}
The HiVT-16 A/B above (Table~\ref{tab:ab16}) is a single training run per arm. To
confirm the gain is not an artefact of one lucky initialisation, the baseline and
the v2 student were each retrained from additional random seeds and re-evaluated
under the identical recipe; Table~\ref{tab:seeds16} reports the resulting
mean$\pm$standard deviation over three converged seeds per arm. The effect is
robust: on the means minFDE falls by $-21.6\%$ ($1.562\to1.224$), and every
calibration metric again moves the right way (mixNLL $-15.7\%$, calibration error
$-19.1\%$). The arms are moreover \emph{cleanly separated on every individual
seed}---the worst v2 run ($1.24$) still beats the best no-KD run ($1.47$) by a wide
margin, so the two seed clouds do not overlap. What the multi-seed view adds---read
cautiously, at $n{=}3$ per arm---is that the no-KD arm looks more
\emph{seed-sensitive}: its three converged seeds span $1.47$--$1.61$ minFDE and a
fourth collapsed outright (excluded below), whereas all three v2 seeds sit in a tight
$1.21$--$1.24$ band. We treat this as suggestive rather than settled---three seeds do
not pin down a variance ratio, and the wide no-KD range is inflated by a single
unusually good seed ($1.47$). What is \emph{firm} is the separation of the two clouds
(the nearest pair, v2 $1.24$ vs.\ no-KD $1.47$, differ by $0.23$\,m); what is only
\emph{suggestive} is that the tighter v2 band points to distillation acting as a mild
training stabiliser (below)---a reading we do not over-claim from so few seeds. The single-run
A/B of Table~\ref{tab:ab16} happened to use the default seed, whose no-KD run
($1.605$) sits near the high (weaker) end of that spread, so its $-22.7\%$ headline
slightly \emph{overstates} the mean gain; the seed-mean $-21.6\%$ is the more
conservative figure and remains a large, reproducible gain.

Evaluating that checkpoint on the full validation set
(Table~\ref{tab:collapse16}) shows precisely \emph{what} the degenerate optimum
is, and it is not a mode collapse: the mixture stays fully populated, with
$\pi$-entropy $1.74$ essentially unchanged from the healthy $1.76$ (both near the

non-converged model, not a sample of the trained-model distribution, and is
reported in that diagnostic context rather than averaged into
Table~\ref{tab:seeds16}.

The pattern across the basin is suggestive. Two full-data routes into it---an
unlucky from-scratch seed and a $3\times$ larger learning rate ($\eta{=}3\mathrm{e}{-}3$,

$b\!\approx\!0.9$ fingerprint (Figs.~\ref{fig:bscale_seeds},~\ref{fig:brier_seeds}),
and the head-starved $8$-head configuration degenerates in the same manner on the
$25\%$ proxy (Section~\ref{ssec:res_heads}, Table~\ref{tab:heads16}); \emph{none}
of the three v2 ($\lambda{=}1.0$) runs entered it. We are careful not to overstate this.
With one collapse among the four from-scratch seeds trained so far, the collapse
\emph{frequency} is only coarsely estimated ($\sim\!1$ in $4$, small-sample), and the
``single basin'' reading rests on the failure \emph{coordinates} clustering at one
characteristic point across these routes rather than on a proof that the optimum
is topologically unique. What we state is the directional observation: the
distillation signal appears to act as a \emph{training stabiliser}, the teacher's
targets supplying a gradient toward the good basin---and specifically toward the
healthy predictive scale---even from an initialisation that would otherwise strand
the small student. Quantifying the collapse rate is a natural thread for more
seeds (Section~\ref{sec:concl_future}).

\paragraph{Why HiVT-32 is not seed-swept.}
The robustness study above is run at HiVT-16 only, which is a deliberate scoping
rather than an oversight. The degenerate basin is a \emph{narrow-width}
pathology: it is reached through configurations that shrink the per-head
dimension or over-drive the optimiser at $\mathrm{embed}{=}16$, and across all
HiVT-32 runs in this chapter the from-scratch model trains stably to
$\approx\!1.16$ minFDE without ever exhibiting the scale-inflated optimum. The
instability that makes a seed sweep \emph{worth} running therefore manifests at
$16$, not $32$, and---given that each full-data seed is a $64$-epoch run---seeds
were concentrated where the variance actually lives. We nonetheless acknowledge
the asymmetry as a limitation: stability at HiVT-32 is supported by a single
converged run plus the recipe-selection sweeps of Section~\ref{sec:res_recipe},
not by a matched multi-seed sample, so it is the weaker, single-sample claim. A
matched HiVT-32 seed sweep is left to future work
(Section~\ref{sec:concl_future}).

\section{Inference Efficiency}
\label{sec:res_eff}

The motivation for a smaller model is cheaper inference. The savings come on two
axes that behave very differently: parameters/memory, which scale cleanly with
width, and latency, which is sublinear and regime-dependent.

\paragraph{Parameters and memory.}
The parameter reduction is exact and unconditional: HiVT-32 and HiVT-16 use $6.6\%$
and $1.8\%$ of the teacher's parameters ($15\times$ and $55\times$ smaller). On GPU
this translates directly into peak memory (Table~\ref{tab:gpumem}): at batch size
$128$ the student-16 uses $543$\,MB against the teacher's $3410$\,MB---a $6.3\times$
reduction---and the ratio \emph{widens} with batch size. This is the clean,
reliable efficiency win: it permits larger batches, smaller GPUs, or co-locating
more models on one device.

\paragraph{Latency.}
Latency tells a more nuanced story (Table~\ref{tab:latency}). On CPU the speedup is
far sublinear in the parameter reduction and depends strongly on batch size: at
$bs{=}1$ (true online inference) HiVT-32 is only $3.0\times$ faster despite $15\times$
fewer parameters, because a fixed $\approx 7$\,ms floor---graph construction, the
fixed transformer-layer count, PyG dispatch, memory-bound scatter/norm ops, all
\emph{independent} of embedding width---dominates the tiny $d^2$ matmuls. Batching
amortises that floor and widens the gap, to $5.5\times$ (HiVT-32) and $11.5\times$
(HiVT-16) around $bs{=}8$--$32$, after which limited CPU parallelism causes
per-scene latency to turn back up. On GPU the latency advantage nearly
vanishes ($\approx 1$--$2.6\times$, occasionally below $1\times$): the GPU has so
much parallelism that the students' small matmuls never saturate it, so the
width-independent overhead dominates and shrinking the model barely changes
wall-clock. The conclusion is that ``$55\times$ smaller'' is a memory claim, not a
speed claim: the unconditional dividend is memory, while the latency win is real
only on CPU under modest batching.

\section{Qualitative and Diagnostic Analysis}
\label{sec:res_qual}

\paragraph{Where in the trajectory distillation helps.}
The headline metrics so far score the entire $3$-second prediction with a single
number, which hides \emph{where along the trajectory} the distillation gain comes
from---whether it shaves a little off everywhere or fixes one hard part. To
resolve this, every prediction is truncated to $1$, $2$, and $3$ seconds and
re-evaluated at each cut-off, re-selecting the best mode by its final-point error
each time; the $1$\,s column thus asks ``how good is the near-future prediction''
on its own, independently of what happens later. Table~\ref{tab:perhorizon}
reports the result, and two patterns stand out. First, error grows steeply with
the horizon for \emph{every} model: predicting the next $3$ seconds is several
times harder than the next $1$, because over a longer window the agent's
intent---turn, lane-change, brake---has more time to express itself and to
diverge from a straight-line continuation. Second, and more useful for this
thesis, \emph{the distillation gain is concentrated in that hard, far-future
tail}. For HiVT-16 the minFDE improvement rises monotonically with horizon, from
$-7.7\%$ at $1$\,s, through $-15.1\%$ at $2$\,s, to $-22.7\%$ at $3$\,s; HiVT-32
shows the same increasing pattern at smaller magnitude
($-3.3\%\rightarrow-7.6\%\rightarrow-9.2\%$). The reading is intuitive: in the
first second the from-scratch student is already close to the teacher and has
little to learn, whereas in the long-horizon tail---exactly where its limited
capacity leaves it most uncertain---the teacher's guidance is worth most.
Distillation therefore does not merely subtract a constant error everywhere; it
specifically repairs the predictions that were worst to begin with.

\paragraph{Predicted multimodality.}
Figure~\ref{fig:traj} shows the six predicted modes for a representative focal
agent at an intersection, ranked by probability, against the observed history and
ground truth. The model spreads probability over distinct, plausible
manoeuvres---differing in reach and curvature---rather than a single averaged
guess, and the highest-ranked modes cover the ground truth well. This is the
behaviour the mixture output and the best-of-$K$ metrics are designed to capture,
and the structure that the permutation-invariant distillation loss is built to
transfer.

\paragraph{The calibration mechanism, directly.}
The diagnostics already embedded in the results sections isolate \emph{why} v1 fails
and v2 succeeds, and they match the analysis of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} point for
point. Across the v1 $\lambda$-sweep the mode-weight entropy is flat while the mean
scale $b$ falls monotonically (Figure~\ref{fig:lambda32}); under v2 the scale
instead returns to the student's calibrated width (Table~\ref{tab:v2_32}). The
over-confidence therefore lives entirely in the per-mode scales, not in the mixture
weights---exactly the signature predicted by Proposition~\ref{prop:v1grad}, whose
stationary-scale formula~\eqref{eq:v1bstar} shows the v1 gradient driving each
student scale toward the (vanishing) residual to the teacher \emph{means}, with no
term opposing $b\to0$. Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} renders the contrast: v1 scores
each teacher mode as a zero-width point, so the student minimises its loss by piling
density on that point and collapsing its scale; v2 scores samples spanning the
teacher's own spread, so the student must stay wide enough to cover them and settles
at $b_S\approx b^T_f$. This is the same statement as the forward-KL reading of
Section~\ref{ssec:method_v2}: v2 minimises $\mathrm{KL}(p_T\Vert p_S)$, which is
mode-covering and so penalises a student too narrow to cover the teacher's mass,
whereas v1---the zero-variance limit---retains only the teacher's locations. A
distribution-matching target is thus not a heuristic patch but the fix the theory
predicts.

\paragraph{Mode diversity.}
A second classic distillation failure---separate from the scale pathology
above---is \emph{mode collapse}: a student that, in trying to imitate the teacher,
quietly abandons its alternative hypotheses and predicts six near-identical
trajectories, throwing away the very multimodality the mixture exists to provide.
Two diagnostics show this does not happen here. The mode-weight entropy is
preserved across the $\lambda$-sweep (above), and Table~\ref{tab:diversity}
measures the \emph{geometric} spread of the modes directly: for each focal agent
it takes the distance between the final points of every pair of the $K{=}6$
predicted modes and averages them (the ``spread'' column), and separately reports
the fraction of those pairs whose endpoints land within $1$\,m of each
other---near-duplicates, the ``collapsed'' column. The distilled students keep a
spread of roughly $5$\,m, essentially the teacher's own value ($5.15$\,m), with
fewer than $2\%$ of mode pairs collapsed. Distillation does \emph{slightly}
tighten the spread relative to the from-scratch model (HiVT-16
$6.37\rightarrow5.45$\,m, HiVT-32 $5.81\rightarrow5.08$\,m), but this is the
intended behaviour, not a defect: v2 pulls the student's modes toward the
teacher's more concentrated, more accurate set, so the student's diversity
\emph{converges toward the teacher's} rather than degenerating toward zero---the
expected behaviour of the mode-covering forward KL of
Section~\ref{ssec:method_v2}, which rewards placing mass on every teacher mode
rather than collapsing onto the dominant one. This
rules out mode collapse---the most common distillation failure, in which a
student mimics only the teacher's dominant mode---and complements the permutation
analysis of Section~\ref{ssec:method_perm}, which established that the teacher and
student modes are mutually unaligned in the first place.

\paragraph{The calibration split during training.}
The final-number tables show v1's over-confidence \emph{at convergence};
Figures~\ref{fig:wandb_dynamics} and~\ref{fig:wandb_dynamics_nll} show it
\emph{emerging throughout training}, on the four metrics logged for all three
matched runs. The two geometry panels
(minFDE, brier-minFDE) confirm that v1 and v2 are indistinguishable and both fall
below the no-KD baseline: the geometric gain is real and identical across the two
objectives. Against that fixed geometry, the two likelihood panels split---v1's
mixture NLL \emph{climbs} above the no-KD baseline (from ${\approx}27$ to
${\approx}38$) while v2's falls below it (to ${\approx}25$), and the same divergence
appears on the best-mode regression NLL, where v1 stalls near $-0.19$ while no-KD
and v2 reach $-0.25$. This is the crux of why mixture NLL diagnoses the
accuracy--calibration trade-off: NLL rewards a good \emph{location} and a matched
\emph{scale} at once, so a rising NLL is ambiguous \emph{in isolation}---but here
the geometry panels rule out a location cause (minFDE is falling in the very same
runs), leaving scale shrinkage below the residual it should report as the only
explanation. The over-confidence of Proposition~\ref{prop:v1grad} is thus visible
\emph{as it happens}, on both the full mixture (mixNLL) and the single best mode
(reg-loss), rather than inferred only from the endpoint.
